Medigap Plan G vs. Plan N

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

With Plan F closed to new Medicare enrollees since January 2020, Plan G has become the gold standard of Medigap coverage — and Plan N has quietly emerged as its most popular cost-conscious alternative. The core question is deceptively simple: is the premium savings from Plan N worth accepting small copays and one specific coverage gap? For many beneficiaries, the math tilts clearly in one direction, but which direction depends on how often you see doctors and whether you ever encounter Part B excess charges.

We break down exactly what each plan covers, what it costs in 2026, and which usage patterns favor each option — with real numbers instead of vague generalizations.

Key Differences Explained

Plan G and Plan N are identical in most respects. Both cover the Part A deductible ($1,676 in 2026), Part A coinsurance for hospital stays, skilled nursing facility coinsurance, Part B coinsurance (the 20% you normally owe on outpatient services), the first three pints of blood, and foreign travel emergency care. Where they diverge comes down to exactly two things: Part B copays and Part B excess charge coverage.

Plan N introduces small copays that Plan G does not: up to $20 for office visits and up to $50 for emergency room visits that do not result in an inpatient admission. These are capped amounts — a routine office visit might carry a $20 copay under Plan N, whereas Plan G would cover it entirely. For a beneficiary who sees a primary care doctor four times a year and two specialists, that is roughly $120 in additional annual out-of-pocket costs under Plan N. Meanwhile, the premium difference between the two plans typically runs $50 to $100 per month — or $600 to $1,200 annually. Simple arithmetic usually favors Plan N for moderate healthcare users.

The less-discussed difference is Part B excess charges. When a doctor does not accept Medicare assignment, they can charge up to 15% above the Medicare-approved amount. Plan G covers this excess; Plan N does not. In practice, about 98% of physicians nationwide accept Medicare assignment, so excess charges are rare. However, they are more common among certain specialists in high-cost areas — orthopedic surgeons, dermatologists, and psychiatrists in Manhattan or Beverly Hills, for example. If you live in an area where non-participating providers are common or you see specialists who are known to charge above Medicare rates, this gap matters. For most beneficiaries, it does not.

Cost Comparison

$150–$250/mo Plan G
$80–$160/mo Plan N

In 2026, Plan G premiums range from about $150 to $250 per month depending on your age, sex, state, and the insurance carrier. Plan N premiums run $80 to $160 per month for the same demographics. Both plans require you to pay the annual Medicare Part B deductible of $257 before Medigap coverage kicks in. The net annual cost difference — accounting for Plan N’s copays — works out to roughly $500–$900 in favor of Plan N for a beneficiary with average healthcare utilization (6 office visits per year). High-utilization beneficiaries (12+ visits) may see that gap narrow to $200–$400 or disappear entirely. Premiums for both plans increase annually; industry data suggests Plan G premiums have risen 6–8% per year recently, while Plan N increases have been slightly lower at 4–6%.

Pros & Cons

Plan G

Advantages

  • Zero out-of-pocket costs beyond the annual Part B deductible ($257 in 2026) — every doctor visit, lab test, and outpatient procedure is fully covered after that
  • Covers Part B excess charges, eliminating the risk of surprise bills from doctors who do not accept Medicare assignment
  • Maximum financial predictability, which is valuable for retirees on fixed incomes who need to budget medical expenses precisely
  • No copays at any point — simplifies paperwork and eliminates the hassle of small bills from multiple providers

Disadvantages

  • Premiums run $50–$100/month higher than Plan N, costing $600–$1,200 more annually regardless of how much healthcare you actually use
  • Premium increases tend to be steeper over time because Plan G attracts older, sicker enrollees who use more services (adverse selection pressure)
  • The comprehensive coverage can insulate beneficiaries from cost awareness, potentially leading to more healthcare utilization than necessary
  • Still requires paying the annual Part B deductible out of pocket ($257 in 2026), which surprises some enrollees who expect truly zero-cost coverage

Plan N

Advantages

  • Premiums typically $50–$100/month lower than Plan G, saving $600–$1,200 annually — savings that compound significantly over a 20+ year retirement
  • Copays are capped at $20 per office visit and $50 per non-admitted ER visit, so worst-case out-of-pocket exposure is modest and predictable
  • Tends to attract a slightly younger, healthier risk pool, which can lead to slower premium increases over time compared to Plan G
  • Covers all the same core benefits as Plan G — hospital stays, skilled nursing, Part B coinsurance — the coverage gap is narrow

Disadvantages

  • Does not cover Part B excess charges, which could result in unexpected bills if you see non-participating Medicare providers
  • Office visit copays of up to $20 add up for beneficiaries with chronic conditions requiring frequent specialist appointments (8+ visits/year)
  • The $50 ER copay for visits that do not result in admission can create hesitation about seeking emergency care
  • Not available with guaranteed issue rights in every situation — switching from Plan G to Plan N after your open enrollment period may require medical underwriting

Who Should Choose What?

Choose Plan G if:

Plan G is the right fit if you have multiple chronic conditions that require frequent specialist visits — think rheumatology, cardiology, oncology follow-ups, and regular imaging. It is also the better choice if you live in an area where some physicians do not accept Medicare assignment, or if you highly value the simplicity of never seeing a medical bill beyond your Part B deductible. For beneficiaries who anticipate heavy healthcare utilization (10+ doctor visits per year, regular outpatient procedures), the premium difference versus Plan N is often offset by the copay savings, making Plan G cost-neutral or even cheaper in high-use scenarios.

Choose Plan N if:

Plan N is the smarter financial pick for generally healthy Medicare beneficiaries who see a doctor 4–8 times per year and rarely visit the emergency room. If you run the numbers — $70/month in premium savings minus $20 copays on perhaps six visits annually ($120) — you still come out $720 ahead with Plan N. That annual savings invested or simply set aside over a 20-year retirement totals $14,400 or more. Plan N is also worth considering for beneficiaries who enrolled in Plan G years ago and are now facing steep premium increases; switching to Plan N (if you can pass underwriting) can reset your premiums significantly. Just confirm that your regular doctors all accept Medicare assignment before making the switch.

The Bottom Line

For most new Medicare enrollees in 2026, Plan N delivers the better value. The coverage is nearly identical to Plan G, the copays are modest and capped, and the premium savings are real and compounding. Plan G earns its higher price tag only for beneficiaries who use healthcare heavily, see non-participating providers, or simply want to eliminate every possible out-of-pocket cost for peace of mind. Before deciding, pull actual quotes from at least three carriers in your zip code — premium differences between insurers for the same standardized plan can be $50/month or more, which sometimes makes Plan G from a low-cost carrier cheaper than Plan N from an expensive one.

Part B excess charges occur when a doctor who accepts Medicare patients but has not signed a participation agreement charges up to 15% above the Medicare-approved amount for a service. For example, if Medicare approves $200 for a specialist visit, a non-participating doctor can charge up to $230, and the $30 excess is your responsibility under Plan N.
