Claiming Social Security at 62 vs. 67 vs. 70

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

The age at which you claim Social Security is arguably the single biggest financial decision of your retirement. For a worker with an average career earnings history, the difference between claiming at 62 and waiting until 70 can mean $150,000 to $200,000 more (or less) in cumulative lifetime benefits depending on how long you live. There is no universally correct answer, but there is almost certainly a correct answer for your situation — and it depends on factors most online calculators ignore, like spousal benefits, tax implications, and whether you plan to keep working.

This analysis lays out the real math, the break-even ages, and the scenarios where early claiming is genuinely smart versus where it is a costly mistake most people come to regret.

Key Differences Explained

For workers born in 1960 or later, full retirement age (FRA) is 67. Claiming at 62 — the earliest possible age — permanently reduces your benefit by 30% compared to your FRA amount. Waiting past 67 earns delayed retirement credits of 8% per year, maxing out at age 70 with a benefit that is 124% of your FRA amount. To put real dollars on this: if your FRA benefit is $2,000/month, claiming at 62 gives you $1,400/month for life, while waiting until 70 gives you $2,480/month for life. That is a $1,080/month difference — $12,960 per year — that persists and grows with annual cost-of-living adjustments for as long as you live.

The break-even math is straightforward but often misunderstood. If you claim at 62 instead of 67, you collect five extra years of reduced payments. Your cumulative benefits at 62 start ahead, but the higher monthly amount at 67 catches up around age 78-79. If you compare 62 versus 70, the break-even age is roughly 80-81. This means: if you live past your early 80s, you come out ahead by waiting. Since the average 62-year-old in reasonable health can expect to live to 84-87, waiting is statistically the winning move for most people — but statistics do not account for individual health conditions, family longevity patterns, or the emotional weight of money in hand today versus a larger check later.

One frequently overlooked factor is the earnings test. If you claim Social Security before your FRA and continue to work, your benefits are temporarily reduced by $1 for every $2 you earn above $23,400 in 2026. At FRA, those withheld benefits are recalculated and added back to your monthly payment, so the money is not lost — but the reduced checks in the interim create cash flow complications that catch many early claimers off guard. After FRA, you can earn unlimited income without any reduction in benefits.

The average Social Security retirement benefit in 2026 is approximately $1,976 per month at full retirement age. Claiming that same benefit at 62 reduces it to roughly $1,383/month — a permanent $593/month reduction. Delaying to 70 increases it to approximately $2,450/month. Over a 20-year retirement (to age 82 if claimed at 62), total benefits collected at 62 equal about $331,920. Claiming at 67 for 15 years yields $355,680. Claiming at 70 for 12 years yields $352,800 — nearly identical to age-67 claiming. But extend the timeline to age 87 (25 years from 62), and the totals diverge dramatically: $414,900 at 62, $474,240 at 67, and $588,000 at 70. The longer you live beyond your early 80s, the more dramatically waiting pays off. For married couples, multiply the survivor benefit impact across the surviving spouse’s remaining lifetime for the full picture.

Pros & Cons

Claim at 62

Advantages

  • Immediate income starting five to eight years sooner — critical if you have been laid off, face health problems, or have depleted savings
  • Provides a financial bridge for early retirees between ages 62 and 65 when Medicare eligibility begins, reducing the need to draw down retirement accounts
  • If you have a shortened life expectancy due to serious health conditions, claiming early maximizes total lifetime benefits received
  • Allows you to preserve other retirement assets (401k, IRA) by drawing Social Security first, potentially letting tax-advantaged accounts grow longer

Disadvantages

  • Permanently reduces your monthly benefit by 30% compared to claiming at full retirement age — this reduction never goes away
  • The earnings test withholds $1 for every $2 earned above $23,400 if you continue working before age 67, creating cash flow complications
  • Lower benefit amount means lower cost-of-living adjustments in dollar terms, compounding the gap over decades of retirement
  • Reduces the survivor benefit your spouse would receive after your death — a particularly costly outcome for the higher-earning spouse in a couple

Claim at 67 or 70

Advantages

  • Claiming at 67 preserves your full benefit; waiting to 70 adds 24% on top of that — translating to hundreds of dollars more per month for life
  • Higher monthly benefit means higher cost-of-living adjustments in absolute dollars, providing better inflation protection over a 20-30 year retirement
  • Maximizes the survivor benefit for a lower-earning spouse, who would receive the higher-earner’s full benefit amount upon their death
  • No earnings test applies after FRA, so you can work without any reduction in benefits — ideal for people who enjoy working into their late 60s

Disadvantages

  • Requires living on other income sources (savings, pensions, part-time work) for 5 to 8 years while waiting for Social Security to begin
  • Break-even age of roughly 80-81 (comparing 62 vs. 70) means the strategy loses if you die before your early 80s
  • Opportunity cost of not investing early Social Security payments — though historically, the 8%/year guaranteed return from delayed credits outperforms most safe investments
  • May force larger withdrawals from tax-deferred accounts (401k/IRA) in the interim, potentially pushing you into a higher tax bracket during those years

Who Should Choose What?

Choose Claim at 62 if:

Claiming at 62 is the right call in a few specific situations. If you have been diagnosed with a serious illness that shortens your life expectancy below the average, collecting early maximizes your total lifetime benefits. It is also defensible if you have lost your job, cannot find employment, and have no other income source to cover basic living expenses. Some financial planners recommend claiming at 62 if you can invest the benefits at returns that exceed the 6-8% annual growth rate of delayed credits — but this requires both market risk tolerance and the discipline not to spend the money. For married couples where both spouses have similar earnings records, having the lower earner claim early while the higher earner delays to 70 is a common and effective strategy that provides income now while maximizing the survivor benefit later.

Choose Claim at 67 or 70 if:

Delaying to 67 or 70 is the stronger move for most people, and it is an especially powerful choice for the higher earner in a married couple. Here is why: the survivor benefit your spouse receives after your death equals your benefit amount at the time of death. If you claim at 62 and receive $1,400/month, that is what your surviving spouse gets. If you wait until 70 and receive $2,480/month, your surviving spouse gets $2,480/month for the rest of their life. For couples with an earnings gap, delaying the higher earner’s benefit is one of the most effective longevity insurance strategies available. Delaying also makes sense if you are in good health with parents who lived into their late 80s or 90s, if you plan to work past 62 and would be subject to the earnings test, or if your other retirement income comfortably covers your expenses until 70.

The Bottom Line

The Social Security Administration is not offering you a gamble — they are offering you actuarially equivalent choices that pay off differently depending on how long you live. If your health is poor or you have no other income, claiming at 62 is reasonable. For everyone else, the math overwhelmingly favors waiting, particularly to 70. The guaranteed 8% annual increase in benefits from delayed retirement credits is nearly impossible to match with any other risk-free investment. And for married couples, the spousal and survivor benefit calculations make delaying the higher earner’s claim to 70 one of the single most valuable moves in all of retirement planning. Run your personal numbers at ssa.gov/myaccount, then talk to a fee-only financial planner — not someone who earns commissions — before locking in your decision.

Spousal benefits and survivor benefits are affected differently. A spousal benefit (collected while both spouses are alive) maxes out at 50% of the higher earner’s FRA benefit amount, regardless of when the higher earner actually claims. However, the survivor benefit — what the surviving spouse receives after the other dies — equals the full amount the deceased spouse was receiving, including any delayed retirement credits. This is why financial planners emphasize delaying the higher earner’s benefit: it directly increases the monthly income the surviving spouse will depend on, often for many years. If the higher earner claims at 62 and receives $1,400/month, the survivor gets $1,400. If they delayed to 70 and received $2,480, the survivor gets $2,480.

The Social Security trust fund reserves are projected to be depleted around 2033-2035 based on the latest Trustees Report. But depletion of the trust fund does not mean benefits disappear. Even with no legislative changes, ongoing payroll tax revenue would still fund approximately 77-80% of scheduled benefits. In other words, the worst-case scenario without any Congressional action is a roughly 20-23% reduction in benefits — not elimination. Historically, Congress has acted to shore up Social Security before reaching crisis points (as it did in 1983). Most policy experts believe some combination of payroll tax increases, benefit formula adjustments, and gradual retirement age increases will be enacted before the trust fund runs out. Avoiding Social Security entirely out of fear that it will not exist is not a sound financial strategy.

If your combined income (adjusted gross income plus nontaxable interest plus half of your Social Security benefits) exceeds $25,000 for single filers or $32,000 for married filing jointly, up to 50% of your benefits may be taxable. Above $34,000 (single) or $44,000 (married filing jointly), up to 85% of benefits become taxable. These thresholds have not been adjusted for inflation since 1993, which means more retirees cross them every year. This is another reason some financial planners recommend delaying Social Security: if you have significant income from other sources between 62 and 70, adding Social Security on top pushes more of it into taxable territory. By waiting, you may be able to draw down taxable accounts first, then start Social Security when your other income has decreased.
