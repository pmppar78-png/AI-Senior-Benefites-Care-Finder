Medicare Advantage vs. Medigap

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

More than 33 million Americans are enrolled in Medicare Advantage plans as of 2026, yet Medigap policies remain the gold standard for predictable out-of-pocket spending. These two paths represent fundamentally different philosophies: Medicare Advantage bundles hospital, medical, drug, and often dental/vision coverage into a single managed-care plan, while Medigap supplements Original Medicare by filling the gaps in Parts A and B—leaving you free to see any Medicare-accepting provider nationwide.

The right pick hinges on how you actually use health care. If you stay close to home, rarely see specialists, and want $0-premium convenience, Advantage plans can be a strong deal. But if you travel frequently, have a complex medical history, or simply want the peace of mind that comes with near-zero surprise bills, a Medigap policy paired with a standalone Part D plan may be worth the higher monthly premium.

Medicare Advantage vs. Medigap — Quick Overview

Medicare Advantage

All-in-one plan with network restrictions

Medigap

Supplement to Original Medicare with more flexibility

Side-by-Side Comparison

Category Medicare Advantage Medigap
Monthly Premium $0–$50 typical $100–$300 typical
Provider Network Network required (HMO/PPO) Any doctor accepting Medicare
Out-of-Pocket Max Yes, capped annually No annual cap (but lower cost-sharing)
Drug Coverage Usually included Requires separate Part D plan

Key Differences Explained

The structural difference is straightforward: Medicare Advantage replaces Original Medicare, while Medigap supplements it. With an Advantage plan, the insurer receives a fixed monthly payment from CMS and manages your care through an HMO or PPO network. You will typically need referrals for specialists (HMO) or pay more for out-of-network visits (PPO). Medigap, by contrast, sits on top of Original Medicare—every doctor or hospital that accepts Medicare assignment accepts your Medigap plan, no network directory required.

Cost exposure differs dramatically. Advantage plans must cap your annual out-of-pocket spending (the 2026 in-network maximum is $8,850), but until you hit that cap you face copays, coinsurance, and sometimes deductibles for each service. Medigap Plan G, the most popular supplement, covers virtually all cost-sharing after you pay the annual Part B deductible ($257 in 2026). That predictability is why Medigap holders report significantly fewer problems with unexpected medical bills.

Prescription drug coverage is another dividing line. Nearly all Advantage plans bundle Part D drug coverage, which simplifies enrollment but means your drug formulary is tied to your medical plan. Medigap policies never include drug benefits; you must purchase a standalone Part D plan separately. This adds a second premium, but it also means you can switch Part D plans every fall without disrupting your medical coverage.

Cost Comparison

$0–$50/mo premium Medicare Advantage
$100–$300/mo premium Medigap

Looking at premiums alone is misleading. A $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan may cost $3,000–$6,000 in copays and coinsurance during a year with a major health event, while a Medigap Plan G holder paying $175/month ($2,100/year) would owe only the $257 Part B deductible for that same event. For a healthy year with just preventive visits, the Advantage enrollee spends near $0 while the Medigap enrollee still pays $2,100+ in premiums. Over a 20-year retirement, Medigap’s total cost tends to be higher for consistently healthy individuals but substantially lower for anyone who experiences a serious illness or injury.

Pros & Cons

Medicare Advantage

Advantages

  • Many plans carry a $0 monthly premium beyond your Part B premium, making them the cheapest entry point
  • Bundled dental, vision, hearing, and often fitness benefits like SilverSneakers reduce the need for separate policies
  • Annual out-of-pocket maximum ($8,850 in 2026) protects against catastrophic costs from a hospitalization or cancer treatment
  • Prescription drug coverage is included in most plans, eliminating the need to shop for a standalone Part D plan

Disadvantages

  • HMO plans require referrals and restrict care to in-network providers; seeing an out-of-network specialist may not be covered at all
  • Prior authorization requirements can delay approval for surgeries, imaging, and certain medications by days or weeks
  • Plans change benefits, formularies, and provider networks every January, which can disrupt ongoing treatments
  • Snowbirds and frequent travelers may face steep out-of-network costs or coverage gaps outside the plan’s service area

Medigap

Advantages

  • See any doctor or hospital in the country that accepts Medicare—no network restrictions, no referrals needed
  • Plan G covers nearly all out-of-pocket costs after the $257 annual Part B deductible, making medical bills highly predictable
  • Guaranteed-renewable for life once enrolled; the insurer cannot drop you or change your benefits regardless of health status
  • Full nationwide coverage makes Medigap ideal for snowbirds, RVers, and anyone who splits time between states

Disadvantages

  • Monthly premiums run $100–$300+ depending on age, location, and tobacco use, plus you still pay the Part B premium
  • Does not include prescription drug coverage—you must buy and manage a separate Part D plan
  • No dental, vision, or hearing benefits are included; you’ll need standalone policies or pay out of pocket
  • Outside the 6-month Medigap Open Enrollment window (starting at age 65), insurers can deny coverage or charge more based on health history

Who Should Choose What?

Choose Medicare Advantage if:

Medicare Advantage tends to work well for people who live in one metro area year-round, have an established network of local doctors already in the plan’s directory, and want the convenience of one card that covers medical, drugs, and extras like dental cleanings and eye exams. It is also the practical choice for anyone on a tight budget who cannot absorb $150–$250 per month in Medigap premiums on top of Part B. Retirees in good health who primarily need preventive care and occasional sick visits will often find that the $0-premium Advantage plan keeps their total annual spending well below what Medigap would cost.

Choose Medigap if:

Medigap makes the most financial sense when you have ongoing specialist care, anticipate surgeries or hospitalizations, or travel extensively within the United States. If your cardiologist, oncologist, or orthopedic surgeon is not in local Advantage networks—or if you split the year between two states—Medigap’s nationwide acceptance eliminates that headache entirely. It is also the stronger pick for anyone who values cost predictability: after the $257 Part B deductible, a Plan G holder pays essentially nothing else for Medicare-covered services, which removes the financial anxiety of a cancer diagnosis or emergency hospitalization.

The Bottom Line

If your retirement budget is tight, your doctors are in-network, and you rarely leave your home region, a well-rated Medicare Advantage plan can deliver solid coverage for very low premiums. But if you can afford the monthly outlay and want ironclad freedom to see any Medicare provider without prior authorization or network worries, Medigap—paired with a standalone Part D plan—remains the most protective option available. The single most important timing consideration: enroll in Medigap during your 6-month open enrollment period starting at age 65, because guaranteed-issue rights disappear afterward in most states.

Frequently Asked Questions

You can leave Medicare Advantage and return to Original Medicare during the annual enrollment period each fall, but purchasing a Medigap policy at that point is not guaranteed. Outside your initial 6-month Medigap open enrollment period (which starts when you turn 65 and enroll in Part B), insurers in most states can deny your application or charge higher premiums based on your health history. A few states like New York, Connecticut, and Massachusetts require guaranteed-issue Medigap enrollment year-round, but most do not. This is one reason many advisors urge seniors to think carefully before choosing Advantage at 65: switching back later can be difficult or expensive.

Most Medicare Advantage plans include some dental and vision benefits, but the scope varies widely. A typical plan might cover two preventive dental visits per year, a basic eye exam, and an eyeglass allowance of $100 to $200. Major dental work like crowns, root canals, or implants is often excluded or subject to separate annual caps of $1,000 to $1,500. Always check the plan’s evidence of coverage document for the specific benefit limits, waiting periods, and network requirements before assuming dental or vision needs are fully handled.

Medicare Advantage HMO plans are required to cover emergency and urgently needed care anywhere in the United States, so an ER visit or emergency hospital admission while traveling will be covered at in-network rates. However, follow-up care after stabilization is typically not covered out of network. If you are admitted to a hospital far from home and need ongoing post-acute care such as rehab or skilled nursing, you may need to transfer back to your plan’s service area or pay out-of-network costs. PPO-type Advantage plans offer more out-of-area flexibility, but at higher cost-sharing. This limitation is a major reason frequent travelers lean toward Medigap.

For someone in average health, Plan G costs roughly $2,100 per year in premiums and covers all Part A and Part B cost-sharing after the $257 annual deductible. A single 3-day hospital stay under Original Medicare without a supplement would cost you the $1,632 Part A deductible alone, plus 20% coinsurance on physician charges. A year with a hip replacement, follow-up therapy, and imaging could easily generate $5,000 to $8,000 in out-of-pocket costs without Medigap. Over time, the premium pays for itself in any year you have a significant health event, and the psychological value of predictable costs is substantial for retirees on fixed incomes.
