Standalone Part D vs. MA Drug Coverage

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

How you get prescription drug coverage under Medicare is shaped by a bigger structural choice: whether you stay in Original Medicare or switch to Medicare Advantage. If you stick with Original Medicare (Parts A and B), you add a standalone Part D plan for drug coverage and can pair it with a Medigap supplement. If you go the Medicare Advantage route, most MA plans bundle drug coverage directly into the plan, designated as MA-PD plans.

Neither path is inherently superior. Standalone Part D gives you the widest possible freedom to pair drug coverage with any Medicare-accepting doctor nationwide, while MA-PD bundles your drug and medical coverage under a single plan with a single card and often a single $0 premium. The tradeoff is between flexibility and simplicity, and the financial math depends heavily on which specific drugs you take, which pharmacies you use, and whether your medications land on favorable formulary tiers.

Key Differences Explained

Standalone Part D plans exist as separate insurance products that layer on top of Original Medicare. You continue to see any doctor who accepts Medicare, and if you also carry a Medigap supplement, your medical cost-sharing is predictable and low. Your drug plan is a separate premium, a separate card, and a separate formulary. In 2026, roughly 900 standalone Part D plans are available across the country, giving you granular control to match a formulary to your exact medication list. The average standalone Part D premium runs about $46 per month, but plans range from $15 to over $80 depending on formulary breadth and gap coverage.

MA-PD plans fold drug coverage into the Medicare Advantage package. You get medical, hospital, and drug coverage from one insurer with one set of rules. Many MA-PD plans advertise $0 premiums, which is technically accurate but somewhat misleading because you still pay the Part B premium of $185 per month regardless. The drug formulary in an MA-PD plan is usually narrower than what you would find in a top-tier standalone Part D plan, and the insurer has less incentive to compete aggressively on drug pricing because the plan is sold primarily on its medical benefits.

The Inflation Reduction Act changes that took full effect in 2025 apply to both pathways equally. The $2,000 annual out-of-pocket cap on Part D drug spending and the $35 monthly insulin copay cap apply whether your drug coverage comes from standalone Part D or MA-PD. However, the way cost sharing accumulates toward that $2,000 cap differs by plan, and some MA-PD plans structure their tiers so that specialty drugs push you to the cap faster.

Standalone Part D premiums in 2026 range from $15 to $80 per month, with the national average near $46. You also pay the Part B premium of $185/month and possibly a Medigap premium of $100 to $250/month, bringing total Original Medicare coverage to roughly $300 to $515 per month before any drug copays. MA-PD plans commonly charge $0 for the combined medical and drug premium beyond Part B, so your total monthly outlay is just the $185 Part B premium. However, MA-PD copays for doctor visits, hospital stays, and prescriptions can add up, and the annual out-of-pocket maximum on medical services can reach $8,850 in-network. Under both options, the Part D annual out-of-pocket cap is $2,000 in 2026 thanks to the Inflation Reduction Act.

Pros & Cons

Standalone Part D

Advantages

  • Roughly 900 plans available nationwide, maximizing your chance of finding one that covers all your medications at preferred tiers
  • Pairs with Original Medicare, so you keep unrestricted access to any Medicare-accepting provider in the country
  • Can switch Part D plans annually without disrupting your medical coverage or Medigap policy
  • Drug-only focus means the plan competes primarily on formulary strength and pharmacy network

Disadvantages

  • Requires a separate monthly premium averaging $46/month on top of Part B and any Medigap premium
  • You manage two or three separate insurance relationships: Medicare, Medigap, and Part D
  • No coordination of medical and drug benefits, so prior authorization processes are handled by different entities
  • Late enrollment penalty of 1% per month applies permanently if you go 63+ days without creditable drug coverage

MA with Drug Coverage

Advantages

  • Single plan, single card, single customer service number for medical and drug coverage combined
  • Drug coverage premium is often $0 because it is subsidized by the overall MA plan design
  • Integrated prior authorization and care management between medical and pharmacy benefits
  • MA-PD plans frequently include extras like mail-order pharmacy with $0 copays on generics

Disadvantages

  • Formularies tend to be narrower since the plan is marketed on medical benefits, not drug selection
  • Changing your drug coverage requires changing your entire medical plan and provider network
  • Network restrictions from the MA plan apply to your medical care, limiting doctor and hospital choice
  • If you later want to leave MA for Original Medicare, you may not pass medical underwriting for a Medigap plan

Who Should Choose What?

Choose Standalone Part D if:

Standalone Part D makes the most sense for beneficiaries who want to stay in Original Medicare and value unrestricted provider choice. It is the clear winner if you take multiple brand-name or specialty medications and need to match a very specific formulary. It also works best when paired with a Medigap supplement, particularly Plan G or Plan N, which together with Part D provide comprehensive and predictable coverage. If you have stable medical needs and care deeply about which drugs are covered at which tier, the ability to shop nearly 900 separate Part D plans gives you unmatched customization.

Choose MA with Drug Coverage if:

MA-PD is the natural fit if you have already decided that Medicare Advantage works for your medical needs and you want to keep everything under one roof. It is especially attractive if you take mostly generic medications, since generics are well covered on nearly all MA-PD formularies and the $0 premium effectively makes your drug coverage free. The integration of medical and pharmacy benefits also helps if you have chronic conditions requiring coordinated care management. Just verify that your specific drugs are on the formulary before enrolling, because switching mid-year is not an option.

The Bottom Line

This decision really comes down to whether you are in Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage, because your drug coverage path follows from that larger choice. If you value seeing any doctor anywhere and are willing to pay more monthly for that freedom, Original Medicare plus a standalone Part D plan plus Medigap is the gold standard for comprehensive, predictable coverage. If you prefer simplicity and lower monthly premiums and your doctors are in a local network, an MA-PD plan bundles everything efficiently. The one non-negotiable step in either case: run your specific medications through the Medicare Plan Finder tool at medicare.gov before enrolling. A plan with a $0 premium is worthless if your $400-per-month brand-name drug is on Tier 4 with 33% coinsurance instead of Tier 2 with a $47 copay.
