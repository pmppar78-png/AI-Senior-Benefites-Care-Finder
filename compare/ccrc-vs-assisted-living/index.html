CCRC vs. Standalone Assisted Living

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

A Continuing Care Retirement Community is not just a place to live — it is a financial and healthcare contract that promises to care for you through every stage of aging, from independent living through skilled nursing, all on a single campus. Standalone assisted living, by contrast, addresses one specific need at one specific time. The decision between them is as much a financial planning question as it is a lifestyle choice, and it typically needs to be made while you are still healthy enough to qualify for CCRC admission.

This guide examines both models honestly — the genuine security a CCRC provides, the real financial risks of entrance fees in the hundreds of thousands of dollars, and the scenarios where each option makes the most sense for your situation and budget.

CCRC (Continuing Care) vs. Standalone Assisted Living — Quick Overview

CCRC (Continuing Care)

Campus with all levels of care

Standalone Assisted Living

Single-level care community

Side-by-Side Comparison

Category CCRC (Continuing Care) Standalone Assisted Living
Entrance Fee $100,000–$500,000 None typically
Monthly Fee $3,000–$5,000 $3,500–$6,000
Continuum Independent → Assisted → Nursing Assisted living only

Key Differences Explained

The fundamental difference is the continuum-of-care guarantee. A CCRC contractually promises that as your needs increase — from needing help with meals and housekeeping, to requiring assistance with bathing and dressing, to needing full-time skilled nursing — you will move within the same campus rather than being discharged to find a new facility. This eliminates what many families describe as the single most stressful event in eldercare: finding and transitioning to a new care setting during a health crisis. Standalone assisted living provides one level of care. When residents need more than the community can offer, they must transfer to a separate skilled nursing facility, often on short notice and under significant stress.

The financial structures are entirely different. CCRCs require a substantial entrance fee — typically $100,000 to $500,000 depending on the community, location, and apartment size — plus a monthly fee of $3,000 to $5,000. Some portion of the entrance fee may be refundable (ranging from 0% to 90% depending on the contract type), and the monthly fee may or may not increase when you move to higher levels of care. Standalone assisted living charges no entrance fee and bills $3,500 to $6,000 per month, but this covers only assisted living services. If you later need memory care (add $1,500–$2,500/month) or skilled nursing ($8,000–$10,000/month), those costs are separate and often at a different facility.

CCRCs also differ in admission timing and requirements. Most require applicants to be relatively healthy and independent at the time of entry, and many have minimum age requirements (typically 62–65). Financial qualification is rigorous — communities want to verify that your assets and income can sustain the monthly fees for your expected lifetime. This means the CCRC decision must be made proactively, often years before care is actually needed. Standalone assisted living has no such advance planning requirement; you move in when you need the care, and admission criteria focus on whether the community can meet your current needs rather than your long-term financial trajectory.

Cost Comparison

$100K–$500K + $3K–$5K/mo CCRC (Continuing Care)
$3,500–$6,000/mo, no entrance fee Standalone Assisted Living

The total cost comparison depends heavily on how long you live and what levels of care you ultimately need. Consider a hypothetical: a 75-year-old enters a CCRC with a $250,000 entrance fee (50% refundable) and pays $4,000/month. Over 10 years, they pay $730,000 total, receive a $125,000 refund to their estate, for a net cost of $605,000. The same person in standalone assisted living at $5,000/month for 5 years, then memory care at $7,000/month for 3 years, then skilled nursing at $9,000/month for 2 years, pays $768,000 total with no refund. In this scenario, the CCRC saves roughly $163,000 over a decade. However, if the person needs only 5 years of assisted living and passes away without needing higher care, standalone at $300,000 total is significantly cheaper. The CCRC is essentially longevity insurance — it pays off if you live a long time and need escalating care.

Pros & Cons

CCRC (Continuing Care)

Advantages

  • Guaranteed access to all levels of care on a single campus — no stressful facility transfers during health crises
  • Monthly fees for higher levels of care are often subsidized relative to market rates, especially under Type A (Life Care) contracts
  • Social continuity: you keep your friends, familiar staff, and community connections even as your care needs change
  • Predictable long-term costs make retirement financial planning more concrete, especially with a Life Care contract that locks in below-market rates for nursing care

Disadvantages

  • Entrance fees of $100,000–$500,000 tie up a large portion of retirement assets in a single illiquid commitment
  • Financial risk if the CCRC operator faces financial distress — several CCRCs have declared bankruptcy in recent years, jeopardizing residents’ entrance fee refunds
  • Must be relatively healthy and financially qualified at admission, so the decision must be made years before care is actually needed
  • Monthly fees still increase annually (typically 3–5%), and some contract types do not cap the cost of higher-level care

Standalone Assisted Living

Advantages

  • No entrance fee preserves capital and keeps your assets liquid for unexpected expenses, family needs, or other investments
  • Flexibility to move to a different community or care setting if your needs, preferences, or finances change
  • No advance health screening or long-term financial qualification — you move in when you need care and meet the current care criteria
  • Month-to-month commitment in most communities, without the long-term contractual obligations of a CCRC

Disadvantages

  • No guarantee of care continuity — if you need skilled nursing or memory care, you must find and transition to a separate facility
  • Total lifetime care costs may exceed a CCRC’s over time, especially if you need memory care ($6,000–$7,000/month) and then skilled nursing ($8,800+/month) at market rates
  • Disruption of social connections and familiar routines when transferring to a new facility during a health decline
  • Families bear the burden of researching, selecting, and coordinating the move to each successive level of care

Who Should Choose What?

Choose CCRC (Continuing Care) if:

A CCRC makes the most sense for retirees who have substantial liquid assets (at minimum $300,000–$500,000 beyond the entrance fee), a reliable income stream from pensions or investments, and a strong preference for planning ahead. Couples who want to age on the same campus — even if one spouse needs skilled nursing while the other remains independent — find particular value in the CCRC model. It is also a strong choice for people without nearby adult children or family members who could coordinate care transitions, since the CCRC handles all of that internally. The ideal time to explore CCRCs is in your mid-to-late 60s, while you are healthy enough to qualify and can make the decision without time pressure.

Choose Standalone Assisted Living if:

Standalone assisted living is the right path for seniors who need care support now and did not plan for (or cannot afford) a CCRC entrance fee. It is also appropriate for families who value flexibility — perhaps you want to try a community before committing, or you may want to relocate closer to family in the future. If your assets are more modest (under $300,000 in liquid savings) or your health does not qualify you for CCRC admission, standalone assisted living gets you the care you need without the financial barriers. Families who choose this route should develop a written plan for what happens if the resident’s needs exceed what assisted living provides, including pre-identifying skilled nursing facilities and understanding Medicaid eligibility rules.

The Bottom Line

A CCRC is one of the few products in eldercare that genuinely reduces risk — the risk of being forced to move during a health crisis, the risk of escalating market-rate care costs, and the risk of family members scrambling to coordinate complex care transitions. But that risk reduction comes at a steep price: six figures upfront, locked into a single organization’s financial health. If you have the resources and the foresight to plan ahead, a CCRC can provide remarkable peace of mind. If your situation demands more flexibility, or if the entrance fee would strain your retirement finances, standalone assisted living with a proactive plan for future care needs is the more prudent choice. Whichever direction you lean, have an elder law attorney review any CCRC contract before signing.

Type A (Life Care) contracts often have declining refund schedules — you might get 90% back if you leave in year one, decreasing to 0% over 4-7 years. Type B (Modified) and Type C (Fee-for-Service) contracts vary widely. Some CCRCs offer a 90% refundable entrance fee option, but the monthly fees are higher to compensate. Most CCRC contracts include provisions for involuntary discharge if a resident’s behavior poses a danger to others, if they fail to pay monthly fees, or if their care needs exceed what the community can provide (though this last scenario is uncommon since CCRCs are designed to handle all care levels). The more realistic concern is a CCRC experiencing financial distress. If the operator cannot sustain operations, residents may face fee increases well above the normal 3-5% annual adjustment, reduced services, or in worst cases, facility closure. Check the CCRC’s financial statements, accreditation status through CARF-CCAC, and any state regulatory filings before committing.

Medicare does not cover CCRC entrance fees or monthly fees for independent or assisted living. Medicare Part A may cover short-term skilled nursing stays within a CCRC’s nursing wing under the same rules that apply to any skilled nursing facility (up to 100 days following a qualifying hospital stay). Medicaid generally does not cover CCRC costs, though the nursing care component may be covered if the resident has spent down assets to Medicaid eligibility levels — this varies by state. Long-term care insurance policies typically reimburse the assisted living and skilled nursing portions of CCRC monthly fees, but do not cover entrance fees or independent living charges. Some CCRC contracts specifically address how long-term care insurance benefits interact with their fee structure.

The ideal window is your early-to-mid 60s. Most CCRCs require applicants to be living independently at admission and will conduct a health screening. Waiting until you already need care support may disqualify you from entry. Many popular CCRCs also have waitlists of 1-3 years for desirable apartment sizes. Starting research at 62-65 gives you time to tour multiple communities, compare contract types, consult financial advisors, have an elder law attorney review contracts, and get on a waitlist if needed — all without the urgency of a health crisis driving the timeline.
