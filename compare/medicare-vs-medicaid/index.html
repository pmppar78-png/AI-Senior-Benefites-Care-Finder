Medicare vs. Medicaid

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

Despite their similar names, Medicare and Medicaid are entirely separate programs with different eligibility rules, funding sources, and benefit structures. The confusion is understandable—both were created in 1965 as part of the same legislation, and roughly 12.3 million Americans (known as “dual eligibles”) qualify for both simultaneously. But for the majority of seniors and people with disabilities, understanding which program applies to your situation—and what each one actually covers—can mean the difference between comprehensive protection and devastating gaps in care.

Medicare is a federal entitlement: you earn it through age (65+) or qualifying disability, regardless of income. Medicaid is a means-tested safety net: you qualify based on limited income and assets, and your state government administers the program with wide latitude over benefits and eligibility thresholds. The two programs overlap most consequentially in long-term care, where Medicare’s coverage is almost nonexistent and Medicaid becomes the largest single payer for nursing home care in America.

Key Differences Explained

Eligibility is the fundamental divide. Medicare eligibility is based on age (65 and older) or disability status (receiving Social Security Disability Insurance for 24 months, or having end-stage renal disease or ALS at any age). Income does not matter—a retiree with $2 million in savings qualifies the same as one with $20,000. Medicaid eligibility is determined by income and assets, with thresholds that vary by state. In 2026, most states set the income limit for seniors at around 100% of the federal poverty level ($15,060/year for an individual), though some expansion states go higher.

Benefits diverge sharply around long-term care. Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay and provides limited home health services, but it does not cover custodial care—the ongoing help with bathing, dressing, and daily living that most nursing home residents need. Medicaid, by contrast, is the nation’s primary funder of long-term care, covering nursing home stays, home and community-based services, and personal care aides for eligible beneficiaries. This single difference drives much of the Medicaid spend-down planning that elder law attorneys specialize in.

Cost-sharing also differs dramatically. Medicare Part B charges a standard monthly premium of $185 in 2026 (higher for high earners), plus the Part A deductible of $1,632 per hospital benefit period, 20% coinsurance on outpatient services, and drug plan copays. Medicaid has little to no cost-sharing—most states charge nominal copays of $1 to $4 per service, and premiums are rare. For dual eligibles who carry both, Medicaid typically pays the Medicare premiums, deductibles, and copays, effectively eliminating out-of-pocket costs.

A typical Medicare beneficiary on Original Medicare pays $185/month for Part B, a Part D drug plan premium of $15–$80/month, and the Part A deductible of $1,632 for each hospital benefit period. Annual out-of-pocket spending averages $6,000–$8,000 for someone with moderate health needs. Add a Medigap Plan G ($150–$250/month) and out-of-pocket costs become far more predictable but total premiums rise to $350–$500/month. Medicaid beneficiaries, by contrast, pay little or nothing—nominal copays of $1–$4 per service in most states, with total annual out-of-pocket spending rarely exceeding $200. Dual eligibles get the best of both: Medicare’s broad provider network with Medicaid picking up all cost-sharing.

Pros & Cons

Medicare

Advantages

  • Available to all Americans 65+ regardless of income, assets, or health status—no means testing
  • Wide provider acceptance: over 97% of non-pediatric physicians accept Medicare patients
  • Choice of Original Medicare with Medigap supplement or Medicare Advantage managed care plans
  • Covers hospital stays, outpatient care, preventive screenings, and prescription drugs (with Part D)

Disadvantages

  • Significant cost-sharing: $1,632 Part A deductible, 20% Part B coinsurance, and Part D drug copays add up quickly
  • No coverage for long-term custodial care in nursing homes or at home beyond short-term skilled nursing
  • Does not cover routine dental, vision, or hearing services under Original Medicare (Parts A and B)
  • Part B premiums are income-adjusted—high earners pay $259 to $594/month via IRMAA surcharges in 2026

Medicaid

Advantages

  • Little to no out-of-pocket cost for covered services, including hospital stays, doctor visits, and prescriptions
  • Comprehensive long-term care coverage including nursing home care, home health aides, and community-based services
  • Covers dental, vision, and hearing services in most states—benefits that Original Medicare lacks
  • Dual-eligible beneficiaries get Medicaid to pay their Medicare premiums and cost-sharing, essentially free coverage

Disadvantages

  • Strict income and asset limits—individuals generally cannot have more than $2,000 in countable assets (though many states have raised or eliminated the asset test)
  • Provider network is more limited; roughly 70% of physicians accept new Medicaid patients compared to 97% for Medicare
  • Benefits and eligibility thresholds vary by state, creating a patchwork system that can change when you move
  • Qualifying for Medicaid long-term care may require spending down life savings, which can affect inheritance plans

Who Should Choose What?

Choose Medicare if:

You don’t choose Medicare in the traditional sense—you’re entitled to it at 65 or upon qualifying disability. The real decision is whether to rely on Medicare alone (with a supplement or Advantage plan) or to also apply for Medicaid if your income and assets are low enough. For middle-income and higher-income retirees, Medicare plus a Medigap policy or Medicare Advantage plan will be the primary coverage. If your retirement income exceeds Medicaid thresholds—roughly $1,255/month for an individual in most states—Medicare is your foundation, and you should plan for cost-sharing through supplemental coverage or personal savings.

Choose Medicaid if:

Seniors with limited income and minimal savings should explore Medicaid eligibility immediately, because Medicaid can eliminate virtually all health care costs. If you receive Supplemental Security Income, you likely auto-qualify in most states. Even if your income is slightly above the threshold, Medicare Savings Programs (QMB, SLMB, QI) can cover your Part B premiums and cost-sharing. The most critical scenario is needing long-term care: without Medicaid, a nursing home stay of $8,800/month will deplete most families’ savings within 1–2 years. Consulting an elder law attorney about Medicaid planning strategies—ideally 3–5 years before you anticipate needing care—can protect assets while establishing eligibility.

The Bottom Line

These are not competing programs—they are complementary ones. Every American who qualifies for Medicare should enroll on time (during the 3-month window before turning 65) to avoid lifetime late-enrollment penalties. If your income and assets also place you within Medicaid thresholds, applying for dual eligibility can wipe out virtually all remaining health care costs. The single most consequential planning gap seniors face is assuming Medicare will cover long-term care. It will not. If you lack long-term care insurance and your savings are modest, understanding Medicaid’s spend-down rules well in advance is the most important financial step you can take for your later years.

Yes, approximately 12.3 million Americans are “dual eligibles” enrolled in both programs simultaneously. When you have both, Medicare acts as the primary insurer for hospital and doctor visits, and Medicaid pays your Medicare premiums, deductibles, and copays. Medicaid also covers services that Medicare does not, such as long-term nursing home care, dental, vision, and extended home health services. Dual eligibles can enroll in Dual Eligible Special Needs Plans (D-SNPs), which are Medicare Advantage plans specifically designed to coordinate both sets of benefits under one plan.

Medicaid spend-down is the process of reducing your countable assets to meet Medicaid’s eligibility threshold, which is $2,000 for an individual in most states (though many states have recently eliminated or raised the asset test). Your home, one vehicle, personal belongings, and certain burial funds are typically exempt. Strategies include paying off debt, making home repairs, purchasing an irrevocable funeral plan, or converting countable assets into exempt ones. There is a 5-year “look-back period” during which Medicaid reviews asset transfers, and gifts or transfers made during that window can result in a penalty period of Medicaid ineligibility. This is why elder law attorneys recommend starting Medicaid planning at least 5 years before you expect to need long-term care.

Medicaid coverage for assisted living varies by state. All states cover nursing home care through their Medicaid programs, but assisted living coverage is only available in states that have approved Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers for that purpose. As of 2026, the majority of states offer some form of HCBS waiver that can help pay for assisted living, but waiting lists are common and can be 12 to 24 months or longer. The amount Medicaid pays for assisted living is typically less than the facility’s private-pay rate, so not all communities accept Medicaid-funded residents. Check your state’s Medicaid agency website for current waiver availability and waitlist status.
