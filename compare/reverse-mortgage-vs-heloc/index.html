Reverse Mortgage vs. HELOC for Seniors

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

Tapping home equity is one of the most consequential financial decisions a retiree can make. Your home is likely your largest asset, and how you access its value will affect your monthly cash flow, your tax situation, the inheritance you leave behind, and whether you can continue living in that home for the rest of your life. The two most common tools for unlocking equity are the FHA-insured Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM), commonly called a reverse mortgage, and a traditional Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC).

These products serve superficially similar purposes but operate on fundamentally opposite mechanics. A reverse mortgage lets you borrow against your equity with no monthly payments required, at the cost of a growing loan balance and significant upfront fees. A HELOC gives you a flexible credit line at lower cost, but demands monthly payments that can strain a fixed retirement income. Neither is inherently better; the right tool depends on whether you need to eliminate monthly payment obligations or minimize long-term borrowing costs.

Key Differences Explained

The most important structural difference is repayment. A HECM reverse mortgage requires no monthly mortgage payments for as long as you live in the home, maintain it, and pay property taxes and insurance. The loan comes due when you move out, sell, or pass away. Interest compounds on the balance over time, meaning the total debt grows every month. A $200,000 reverse mortgage at 6.5% interest would grow to roughly $370,000 after 10 years and over $680,000 after 20 years, assuming no payments are made. A HELOC, by contrast, functions like a credit card secured by your home: you draw funds as needed during a 10-year draw period, make interest-only or principal-plus-interest payments monthly, and then repay the full balance during a 20-year repayment period.

Costs at origination also differ dramatically. A HECM carries an upfront mortgage insurance premium (MIP) of 2% of the home's appraised value, an origination fee of up to $6,000, and standard closing costs. On a $400,000 home, total upfront costs often reach $12,000 to $18,000. HELOCs typically cost $0 to $2,000 to open, with many lenders waiving closing costs entirely as a promotional offer. This cost gap means the reverse mortgage starts you in a deeper financial hole from day one.

The protections built into each product are notably different. HECM borrowers benefit from FHA insurance guaranteeing they can never owe more than the home's value when the loan comes due, even if the balance has grown to exceed it. This non-recourse protection means your heirs cannot be pursued for any shortfall. HELOCs offer no such protection, and lenders can freeze or reduce your credit line if your home value drops or your financial situation changes. In the 2008 financial crisis, millions of HELOC holders had their credit lines slashed or frozen with little warning.

Cost Comparison

$12K–$18K upfront Reverse Mortgage (HECM)
$0–$2K upfront HELOC

A HECM reverse mortgage on a $400,000 home in 2026 typically carries upfront costs of $12,000 to $18,000, including the 2% initial MIP ($8,000), origination fee (up to $6,000), and closing costs. The ongoing interest rate averages 6.0% to 7.5% for fixed-rate and 5.5% to 7.0% for adjustable-rate HECMs, plus an annual MIP of 0.5%. Over 15 years with no payments, a $200,000 draw grows to approximately $500,000 in total debt. A HELOC in 2026 typically costs $0 to $2,000 upfront, with variable interest rates averaging 7.5% to 9.5%. Monthly payments on a $100,000 HELOC balance run approximately $625 to $790 interest-only during the draw period. The total interest paid on a HELOC is dramatically lower because you are paying it down monthly, but the monthly payment obligation is the tradeoff you accept.

Pros & Cons

Reverse Mortgage (HECM)

Advantages

  • No monthly mortgage payments required, which can free up $500 to $2,000+ per month for retirees on fixed income
  • Non-recourse FHA insurance guarantees you and your heirs will never owe more than the home is worth
  • Loan proceeds are generally not considered taxable income, preserving your tax bracket and benefit eligibility
  • Can be structured as a line of credit that grows over time, effectively increasing your available borrowing capacity

Disadvantages

  • Upfront costs of $12,000 to $18,000 on a typical home, including the 2% FHA mortgage insurance premium
  • Interest compounds on the full balance, and a $200,000 draw can grow to $370,000+ in just 10 years
  • Reduces or eliminates the equity available for heirs, which can create family tension around inheritance expectations
  • Must maintain the home, pay property taxes, and keep homeowner's insurance current or risk foreclosure

HELOC

Advantages

  • Very low upfront costs, typically $0 to $2,000 with many lenders offering no-closing-cost promotions
  • Interest rates are often 1 to 2 percentage points lower than reverse mortgage rates in 2026
  • You only pay interest on the amount drawn, and repaying principal immediately stops interest on that portion
  • Preserves more equity over time because monthly payments prevent the compounding debt spiral

Disadvantages

  • Monthly payments of $300 to $1,200+ depending on the balance, which can be burdensome on a retirement income
  • Lender can freeze or reduce your credit line at any time if property values decline or your credit deteriorates
  • Draw period typically ends after 10 years, after which you enter mandatory repayment and can no longer borrow
  • Variable interest rates can increase your monthly payment significantly if rates rise during the loan term

Who Should Choose What?

Choose Reverse Mortgage (HECM) if:

A reverse mortgage fits retirees who need to eliminate monthly housing payments to make their fixed income work, plan to stay in their home for at least 7 to 10 years to justify the high upfront costs, and are less concerned about maximizing the inheritance they leave behind. It is particularly valuable for seniors whose Social Security and pension income is modest but who have significant equity in a paid-off or nearly paid-off home. The HECM line of credit option is also a sophisticated financial planning tool: the unused portion grows at the loan's interest rate plus 0.5%, giving you an expanding reserve fund for future care needs.

Choose HELOC if:

A HELOC works best for retirees who have reliable monthly income to handle payments, need access to equity for a defined short-term purpose like home renovations or bridge financing, and want to minimize borrowing costs. It is the better choice if you plan to sell your home within the next 5 to 7 years since the lower upfront costs and lack of compounding debt will leave you with more net proceeds at sale. A HELOC is also preferable for retirees who want to preserve as much equity as possible for their heirs or who may need to tap equity intermittently rather than as a primary income supplement.

The Bottom Line

The reverse mortgage vs. HELOC decision boils down to one core question: can you afford monthly payments, or do you need the payment-free structure a reverse mortgage provides? If you can comfortably handle HELOC payments, you will almost always come out ahead financially because the lower costs and interest-as-you-go structure preserves far more equity. But if monthly payments would strain your budget and jeopardize your ability to cover essentials like food, medication, and property taxes, the reverse mortgage's no-payment structure is not just a convenience; it is a genuine lifeline. Whichever path you consider, consult with a HUD-approved housing counselor, which is mandatory for HECM applicants and free for anyone. These counselors can run the numbers specific to your home value, age, interest rates, and financial goals, and they are required by law to be neutral rather than steer you toward either product.

The servicer is required to give you notice and an opportunity to cure defaults before starting foreclosure. Roughly 10% of reverse mortgage borrowers have faced default proceedings, most commonly due to unpaid property taxes.

Your heirs have 30 days after notification from the servicer to decide what to do, and up to 6 months (with possible extensions to 12 months) to settle the loan. They can sell the home and keep any equity above the loan balance, pay off the loan and keep the home, or simply walk away if the loan exceeds the home's value. Due to HECM's non-recourse protection, heirs are never personally responsible for any shortfall between the loan balance and the home's sale price. FHA insurance covers the difference.

Neither HELOC draws nor reverse mortgage proceeds count as income for Social Security or Medicare purposes, so they will not increase your Part B premium or trigger taxation of Social Security benefits. However, if you receive SSI or Medicaid, funds sitting in your bank account from either source could be counted as assets and jeopardize your eligibility if they push you over the $2,000 resource limit. Spend the funds in the same calendar month they are received to avoid this issue.
