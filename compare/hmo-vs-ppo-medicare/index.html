Medicare HMO vs. PPO Plans

Updated May 9, 2026 2026 Comparison

When you enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan, you will almost certainly encounter two dominant plan types: HMO and PPO. Together they account for over 90% of all Medicare Advantage enrollment nationwide, yet they impose very different rules on how you access care. An HMO channels your care through a defined provider network and requires referrals to see specialists, while a PPO lets you visit out-of-network doctors at higher cost sharing without needing anyone's permission first.

This is not a trivial distinction. For a retiree in a metro area with strong network options, an HMO can deliver outstanding value with low premiums and tight care coordination. But for someone who splits time between two states, sees specialists at an academic medical center outside any insurer's network, or simply values the freedom to make their own care decisions, a PPO's flexibility may be worth every extra dollar. The right answer depends on where you live, how you use medical care, and how much weight you place on predictable costs versus open access.

Key Differences Explained

Network rules are the headline difference, but the downstream effects are what actually shape your experience. In a Medicare HMO, you must select a primary care physician (PCP) who acts as a gatekeeper. Seeing a cardiologist, orthopedic surgeon, or any other specialist requires a referral from that PCP. If you visit a provider outside the HMO network for anything other than emergency or urgent care, Medicare Advantage pays nothing and you shoulder the entire bill. This constraint works as a feature for some people because it forces care coordination. Your PCP tracks your full medical picture and can catch conflicting medications or redundant tests.

Medicare PPOs eliminate both the PCP gatekeeper and the network boundary, though they do not eliminate the cost difference. You can self-refer to any specialist and you can see out-of-network providers, but the cost-sharing gap is significant. An in-network specialist visit in a PPO might carry a $30 copay, while the same visit out-of-network could cost you $60 to $80 or a 40% coinsurance rate. Out-of-network claims also typically face a separate, higher maximum out-of-pocket (MOOP) limit, which can reach $11,300 in 2026 compared to $8,850 for in-network services.

Premium differences reflect these structural gaps. In 2026, many Medicare HMOs offer $0 monthly premiums in competitive markets, especially in Florida, California, Texas, and parts of the Northeast. Medicare PPOs in those same counties average $25 to $55 per month. That premium gap compounds over a year and often widens further when you factor in the higher copays and coinsurance that PPOs charge for out-of-network usage. However, PPOs frequently include richer supplemental benefits like expanded dental and vision coverage or higher allowances for over-the-counter health products.

Cost Comparison

$0–$25/mo HMO Plan
$25–$75/mo PPO Plan

In 2026, Medicare HMO premiums average $0 to $25 per month in most metropolitan areas, with in-network primary care copays of $0 to $15 and specialist copays of $20 to $45. The in-network maximum out-of-pocket ranges from $4,000 to $8,850 annually. Medicare PPO premiums typically run $25 to $75 per month, with in-network copays similar to HMOs but out-of-network visits costing 30% to 40% coinsurance. PPOs carry a combined MOOP that can reach $8,850 in-network and $11,300 out-of-network. Over the course of a year, a healthy enrollee might save $300 to $900 in premiums alone by choosing an HMO, while a PPO enrollee who uses out-of-network services could face $1,500 to $3,000 in additional cost sharing.

Pros & Cons

HMO Plan

Advantages

  • Lowest premiums available, often $0/month in competitive markets for 2026
  • Coordinated care through a primary care physician reduces fragmented treatment and medication conflicts
  • Lower copays for in-network services, typically $0 to $15 for primary care visits
  • Single in-network MOOP limit of $4,000 to $8,850 keeps worst-case costs predictable

Disadvantages

  • Zero coverage for out-of-network providers except in true emergency or urgent-care situations
  • Referral requirement adds delays when you need to see a specialist quickly
  • Limited plan availability in rural areas where network adequacy is harder to achieve
  • Snowbirds and frequent travelers may find their network useless outside their home service area

PPO Plan

Advantages

  • Freedom to see any Medicare-accepting doctor without a referral, in or out of network
  • Nationwide coverage makes PPOs practical for retirees who travel or maintain homes in multiple states
  • Out-of-network access provides a safety valve when the closest specialist is not in any local network
  • Often includes richer supplemental benefits such as higher dental and vision allowances

Disadvantages

  • Monthly premiums typically run $25 to $55 higher than comparable HMOs in the same county
  • Out-of-network cost sharing of 30% to 40% can produce large surprise bills for procedures
  • Separate and higher out-of-network MOOP limit of up to $11,300 in 2026 increases financial exposure
  • Lack of required care coordination means you must manage referrals and records yourself

Who Should Choose What?

Choose HMO Plan if:

A Medicare HMO is the stronger choice if you live year-round in one metro area with robust provider networks, your preferred doctors and hospital are already in-network, and you appreciate having a primary care physician coordinating your care. It is especially compelling for budget-conscious retirees who want predictable, low-cost copays and have no need to see out-of-network specialists. Many HMOs in competitive markets offer $0 premiums with drug coverage included, making them hard to beat on pure value.

Choose PPO Plan if:

A Medicare PPO makes sense if you split your time between states, travel frequently, or have established relationships with specialists who do not participate in local HMO networks. It is also the better fit if you have a complex medical situation requiring multiple specialists and you want the ability to self-refer without waiting for PCP approval. Retirees who value autonomy in their healthcare decisions and are willing to pay somewhat higher premiums and copays for that freedom will find the PPO structure much less restrictive.

The Bottom Line

The HMO vs. PPO decision is ultimately about how much flexibility is worth to you in dollar terms. If you can live within a network, the HMO will almost always cost less and may actually produce better health outcomes through coordinated care. But if your medical needs, travel patterns, or personal preferences demand the ability to see any provider anywhere, the PPO's premium surcharge is the price of that freedom. One practical test: pull up the provider directory for the most popular HMO in your county and check whether your current doctors, preferred hospital, and any specialists you see regularly are all listed. If yes, the HMO is likely your best value. If even one critical provider is missing, a PPO may save you from far more expensive out-of-pocket surprises down the road.

Frequently Asked Questions

You can switch Medicare Advantage plans during the Annual Enrollment Period from October 15 to December 7 each year, with your new plan taking effect January 1. There is also an Open Enrollment Period from January 1 to March 31 where current Medicare Advantage members can make one switch to another MA plan or return to Original Medicare. Outside these windows you generally cannot change plans unless you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period due to moving, losing employer coverage, or other qualifying life events.

HMOs cover emergency and urgently needed care anywhere in the United States regardless of network. However, routine care received outside your plan's service area is not covered. If you spend winters in another state for several months, your HMO network back home is essentially useless during that time. Some HMOs offer visitor or guest programs with limited out-of-area coverage, but these are uncommon and typically capped at 30 to 90 days.

A Point-of-Service plan is a hybrid that works like an HMO for in-network care but allows some out-of-network visits at higher cost, similar to a PPO. POS plans are relatively rare in Medicare Advantage, accounting for less than 5% of plans nationally. They still require a primary care physician and referrals for in-network specialist visits but provide partial out-of-network coverage. If you like the HMO structure but want an emergency escape valve for occasional out-of-network access, a POS plan splits the difference.

Both HMO and PPO Medicare Advantage plans can include Part D drug coverage, and most do. The formulary, tier structure, and pharmacy networks vary by individual plan rather than by plan type. However, PPOs tend to have broader pharmacy networks simply because the plan structure allows out-of-network pharmacies. In practice, major chain pharmacies are included in most MA drug plans regardless of type. The key factor is whether your specific medications are on a given plan's formulary at a favorable tier, which you should check on Medicare.gov's Plan Finder tool.
