SNAP for Seniors: Find Your State

SNAP is a federal program, but the state you live in decides a surprising amount: which office takes your application, whether your savings are counted, and how high your income can go before you are screened out. This page sets out the national numbers USDA published for the year that began October 1, 2026, then shows how each state's rules differ, so you can open the right state guide with the right expectations.

National figures are from USDA pages read on November 15, 2026. The state column uses USDA's broad-based categorical eligibility chart, updated November 12, 2026.

What changes when someone in the home is 60 or older

In SNAP, elderly means 60 or older, and USDA's special rules page lists the disability payments, such as SSI or Social Security disability, that also qualify a member as disabled. Those households get four breaks that most families do not:

  • They only have to pass the net income test, not the gross one.
  • Out-of-pocket medical costs above $35 a month, including prescriptions, dentures, health insurance premiums and some transportation, are deducted.
  • Shelter costs above half of adjusted income can be deducted with no $769 cap.
  • Work requirements do not apply when every member of the household is elderly or disabled.

Two living situations get special treatment too. A person 60 or older who cannot buy and prepare meals separately because of a permanent disability may form a separate household with a spouse, provided the others in the home have income no higher than 165 percent of poverty ($2,195 a month for one person in FY2027). And residents of federally subsidized housing for the elderly can qualify even when the building provides meals.

Why the state matters: broad-based categorical eligibility

Under broad-based categorical eligibility, or BBCE, a state ties SNAP to a non-cash benefit funded by TANF, often just a brochure, which lets it set its own asset and gross income limits. USDA's BBCE chart lists 46 states and territories using it. In most of them there is no asset test at all. USDA adds that BBCE cannot shrink eligibility: a household that does not fit the state's BBCE group can still qualify under the regular federal rules.

Before you open your state's page

You have to apply in the state where you currently live, and each state uses its own form. USDA's eligibility page says most decisions come within 30 days, with benefits paid back to the application date. Its rules for elderly and disabled households add a faster track: a household with under $100 in liquid resources and under $150 in monthly gross income, or whose rent and utilities exceed its income and cash combined, may get benefits within seven days.

If you cannot get to an office or online, you can name an authorized representative in writing to apply and be interviewed for you. And if you disagree with a decision, you have 90 days to ask for a fair hearing.

Frequently asked questions

Which states have no asset test for seniors?

Every state on USDA's BBCE chart listed with “no limit on assets” in the table above. The nine states not on the chart (Idaho, Indiana, Kansas, Mississippi, Missouri, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah and Wyoming) use the federal $4,750 limit for a household with a member 60 or older or disabled. Arkansas, Nebraska and Texas set their own BBCE limits: $5,500, then $4,500, for elderly or disabled households in Arkansas, $25,000 in liquid assets in Nebraska, and $5,000 in Texas. Louisiana applies the SNAP limit, $4,750 with a 60+ or disabled member, since November 15, 2026.

Is the 130 percent gross limit the one that applies to me?

Not if your household has someone 60 or older; federal rules only apply the net test then. Many states also use a higher BBCE gross limit.

Do Alaska and Hawaii use the same numbers?

No. USDA sets higher income limits and benefit amounts for Alaska and Hawaii.
