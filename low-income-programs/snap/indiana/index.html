Indiana SNAP for Older Hoosiers

In Indiana, food assistance runs through the Family and Social Services Administration's Division of Family Resources (DFR), which applies the federal SNAP rules in every county. If you are 60 or older, a few of those rules work in your favor, and Indiana adds one of its own on vehicles. This page walks through the numbers that took effect on October 1, 2026, and what DFR asks of older applicants.

Figures on this page were copied from FSSA and USDA pages on November 15, 2026. They apply to federal fiscal year 2027 (October 1, 2026, through September 30, 2027).

Who runs SNAP in Indiana

SNAP is a federal program paid for by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, but benefits are handed out by states. Indiana gives that job to FSSA's Division of Family Resources. DFR is responsible for applying federal rules consistently in each county, and its workers handle the application, the eligibility interview and the notice that follows. The card that carries the benefit is called the Hoosier Works EBT card.

Applicants also have to meet non-financial rules: Indiana residency, citizenship or eligible non-citizen status, and work registration with IMPACT, the state's SNAP job-training program. Under the federal law signed on July 4, 2025, FSSA says the upper age exception to the time-limited work rule for adults without dependents rose to 65 and older, and DFR has put that change in place. Someone aged 60 to 64 who lives alone and is able to work should ask the caseworker whether the time limit applies.

Income limits in effect since October 1, 2026

Most Indiana households must pass two tests, gross income and net income. A household with a member who is 60 or older, or who is disabled, only has to pass the net test. That matters for retirees whose Social Security check sits a little above the gross line: once DFR subtracts allowable costs, the net figure is what decides eligibility.

If everyone in the household receives Supplemental Security Income or TANF cash assistance, DFR says the income limits do not apply at all.

What Indiana counts as an asset

DFR sets the asset limit at $4,750 for an assistance group that includes someone 60 or older or disabled, compared with $3,000 for other groups. Bank accounts, cash, real estate other than the home, and personal property can count.

Several things are left out. The home and the lot it sits on are excluded, and so are household goods, personal belongings and life insurance policies. Indiana goes further than the federal baseline on cars: any vehicle used for household transportation is exempt regardless of its value, license or mechanical condition. Only recreational vehicles, or vehicles not used to get the household around, have their equity counted.

Deductions that help older applicants

Net income is gross income minus deductions. For FY2027 USDA sets the standard deduction at $217 for households of one to three people. On top of that, a household member who is 60 or older can deduct out-of-pocket medical costs above $35 a month, as long as Medicare, Medicaid, other insurance or someone outside the household is not paying them. Prescriptions, dental bills, dentures, hospital charges, health insurance premiums and certain medical transportation are among the costs USDA lists.

DFR asks for proof of those bills, so bring pharmacy printouts, premium notices and receipts. The shelter deduction is also more generous for older households: USDA lets them deduct all shelter costs above half of their adjusted income, with no $769 cap. Internet bills no longer count toward shelter costs, a change FSSA says it has already made.

How much an Indiana household can receive

The benefit is the maximum allotment for your household size minus 30 percent of net income. Indiana uses the same table USDA sets for the 48 contiguous states and DC, and DFR publishes it alongside its income limits.

USDA sets a minimum of $25 a month for eligible one- and two-person households in FY2027. DFR stresses that the maximum is what a household with zero net income gets, so most older applicants with Social Security income receive less.

Applying through DFR

The quickest route is the FSSA Benefits Portal, where you can file online. FSSA's SNAP page does not describe a separate short form for older adults, so seniors use the same application as everyone else. Anyone without a computer can apply at a county DFR office; the local office finder lists them by county.

Expect to give names, birth dates and Social Security numbers for everyone in the home, plus proof of income, resources, rent or mortgage and utilities. After the interview, DFR mails a form listing anything still missing, with a due date. A household with gross income under $150 and less than $100 in cash, checking and savings can get benefits within seven days; so can one whose housing costs are more than its income and cash combined. Everyone else should get a decision within thirty days.

If you cannot handle the paperwork yourself, USDA allows an authorized representative, named in writing, to apply and be interviewed for you. For FFY 2027 FSSA lists Feeding Indiana's Hungry, Food Finders Food Bank, Gleaner's Food Bank, Food Bank of Northwest Indiana and Eskenazi Health as its SNAP outreach providers.

Using the Hoosier Works card

Once approved, DFR orders a Hoosier Works card automatically and it normally arrives within three to five business days. Returning recipients keep their old card. Deposits follow the first letter of the cardholder's last name, starting on the 5th of the month for A and B and ending on the 23rd for W through Z.

The card works at USDA-approved grocers, co-ops and farmers markets, and Indiana allows online grocery orders for pickup or delivery, though SNAP cannot pay the delivery fee. One rule changes soon: starting November 23, 2026, FSSA will remove any benefits that are at least 274 days old, about nine months, and expunged benefits cannot be replaced. A senior who has been saving a balance should plan to spend it before then.

Frequently asked questions

Does my Social Security count as income for Indiana SNAP?

Yes. DFR counts Social Security, SSI, pensions, unemployment and most other income, though some unearned income is exempt under federal law. Because a household with someone 60 or older only has to pass the net income test, medical and housing deductions can still bring you under the $1,330 net limit for one person.

Will DFR count my car against the asset limit?

Not if the household uses it for transportation. Indiana exempts those vehicles whatever their value; only recreational vehicles or ones not used to get around are counted.

What happens if I disagree with DFR's decision?

You can appeal in person, by phone or in writing to DFR. The appeal goes to a hearing before the Office of Administrative Law Proceedings, and USDA says the request must be made within 90 days of the decision.
