Food and Nutrition Services for Older North Carolinians

North Carolina rarely calls it SNAP. The state name is Food and Nutrition Services, or FNS, and many people still say food stamps. NCDHHS sets policy, while your county Department of Social Services (DSS) handles the case. For people on SSI who are 65 or older, the state also runs a separate, simpler program that can skip most of the math.

Every figure below was compared with NCDHHS and USDA pages on November 15, 2026. The federal amounts belong to FY2027, which started October 1, 2026, and ends September 30, 2027.

Two ways an older adult can get food help

Most seniors go through regular Food and Nutrition Services, which follows federal SNAP rules. North Carolina also operates a demonstration project that NCDHHS calls the Simplified Nutritional Assistance Program. Confusingly, it is abbreviated SNAP too, but it is a different, narrower benefit aimed at elderly SSI recipients.

You do not have to pick one. NCDHHS says anyone who applies for regular FNS is screened for the simplified program and placed in it if they qualify and the simplified amount is equal to or higher than what regular FNS would pay.

The Simplified Nutritional Assistance Program

To qualify, a person must receive SSI (other income is allowed as well), be 65 or older, live in North Carolina outside an institution, buy and prepare food separately from others in the home, and not get food benefits in another state. Every case is a one-person household, and married people living together cannot be treated as separate households.

Income and deductions play no part in the amount. Only rent, mortgage or lot rent does.

NCDHHS identifies likely participants each month through the North Carolina State Data Exchange (SDX) match and mails them an application. Approved cases are certified for 36 months, a long stretch that spares older adults frequent renewals. The NCDHHS page did not give an effective date for these amounts as of November 15, 2026, so confirm the current figure with your county DSS.

Regular FNS: no asset test for most households

North Carolina uses what USDA calls broad-based categorical eligibility, or BBCE. On its state chart, updated November 12, 2026, USDA lists North Carolina as making all households eligible through a statement on the application and recertification forms, with no limit on assets and a gross income limit of 200 percent of poverty. A household at or under that line is categorically eligible, so savings, a paid-off car or a retirement account do not block it. That fits the NCDHHS page, which tells applicants they may still qualify if they own a home or car.

USDA's chart gives the 200 percent limit as a percentage only. The dollar rows above are twice USDA's FY2027 100 percent figures, which are the net limits shown underneath them.

Being categorically eligible is not the same as receiving a payment. The amount is still the maximum, $306 for one person, minus 30 percent of net income, so income after deductions has to be low enough to leave something. Households above 200 percent can still apply under the regular federal rules, where a household with someone 60 or older skips the gross test but must meet the net limit, and the $4,750 resource limit applies.

Deductions and benefit size

Caseworkers subtract a standard deduction, set by USDA at $217 for one to three people in FY2027, and medical costs above $35 a month for members who are 60 or older or disabled. Shelter costs above half of the household's remaining income can be deducted in full for older households, without the $769 cap that applies to others. That is why NCDHHS tells applicants to bring medical bills for anyone over 60.

USDA's FY2027 maximum is $306 a month for one person and $562 for two, minus 30 percent of net income. On November 15, 2026, the benefit table on the NCDHHS FNS page still listed $298 for one person and $546 for two, matching the previous year. Rely on USDA's FY2027 figures for the current maximum, and on your DSS notice for your own amount.

Applying through ePASS or your county DSS

There are three routes. You can apply online with ePASS, which may ask you to create an account first; drop off or complete a paper application at your county DSS office; or mail the paper form to that office. The local DSS directory lists every county.

NCDHHS urges people not to wait for a perfect file. Benefits begin from the day the application arrives, even if it is incomplete, and missing documents can be added later. The checklist covers ID for the head of household, Social Security numbers and birth dates, bank and retirement balances, monthly bills, proof of income, paperwork from Medicaid or SSI, and medical bills for anyone over 60 or disabled.

After an online filing, a caseworker usually calls within a few days to set up the interview, which can be in person, by phone or by video. You may send someone you choose to be interviewed for you. Processing can take up to 30 days. Recertification and change reports can now be submitted online as well.

Recent federal changes and card basics

NCDHHS reports two changes from the July 4, 2025, federal law: expanded work requirements effective December 1, 2025, and new non-citizen eligibility rules effective February 1, 2026. Your county DSS screens for both at your next recertification. Households made up entirely of elderly or disabled members are not subject to SNAP work requirements, according to USDA.

Benefits land on an EBT card on a fixed day set by the last digit of your Social Security number, from the 3rd of the month for a 1 through the 21st for a 0, available after 6 a.m. Balances can be checked online through ebtEDGE or its mobile app. The card cannot buy hot prepared foods, vitamins, medicines or pet food.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to apply separately for the simplified program?

No. NCDHHS mails an application to likely participants found through the State Data Exchange match, and regular FNS applicants are checked for it automatically. Existing FNS cases that qualify are converted when the simplified amount is equal or higher.

My spouse and I are both on SSI. Can we each get the simplified benefit?

Not as separate households. NCDHHS says married people living in the same home cannot be treated as separate households under the simplified program, so a couple goes through regular FNS.

Why does the NCDHHS benefit table differ from USDA's?

The state table had not caught up with the October 1, 2026, update; the section on deductions and benefit size gives the current USDA amounts.

Will my savings stop me from qualifying in North Carolina?

Usually not. With gross income at or below 200 percent of poverty, USDA lists no asset limit for North Carolina; only households above that line face the federal $4,750 limit for a member who is 60 or older or disabled.
